# Space bindings: how the workspaces of a space call each other

A space gives people roles on its workspaces (docs/spaces.md). It gives the workspaces themselves nothing: two workspaces of one space cannot call each other. This record proposes how they will, modelled on the binding edges of plans/multi-gadget.md. It is a design only and changes no code; the names it introduces (`SpaceBindingGatekeeper`, `SpaceHandler`) are placeholders. Throughout, workspace A calls a gadget of workspace B.

## Current state

- **A binding is an edge on the record that holds it.** `GadgetRecord.bindings` maps a name to a `BindingRecord`, whose `target` is a `WorkpieceId` of the same workspace (`storage-schema/overseer-storage.ts`). `OverseerImpl.bindWorkpiece` takes only a gatekeeper record as the target: it refuses a worktree, and a gadget with "Gadget-to-gadget bindings are not supported yet." Only an agent spawner's edges (`GatekeeperRecord.bindings`) and a chat's bindings (`getEnvForAgent`) name gadgets, so no gadget's env holds another gadget.
- **Every entry of a gadget's env is a `GatekeeperLoopback`** (`getEnvForLoader`): `GADGET`, `GIT` and one per visible edge. Its props are the Overseer's id, a `BindingLoopbackTarget` and a `GatekeeperCaller`, and every call on it runs `startGatekeeperSession(target, caller)` afresh. For a gatekeeper target, that is `openGatekeeperSession`, which hands `Gatekeeper.startSession` an `ApprovalQueueImpl` tied to that gatekeeper and caller. Through the queue a read is an `authorizeObservation` and a write a `submitAction`, each an `ActionRecord` under the gatekeeper's id.
- **A gatekeeper can be the kernel's own.** `AgentSpawnerGatekeeper` is a class the kernel's Worker exports, minted with props and stored through `addGatekeeper` under the creation spec `{type: "agentSpawner"}` (`newAgentSpawnerGatekeeper`). `openGatekeeperSession` gives its sessions a queue that can also start agents (`AgentSpawnerQueueImpl`). It has no vendor, so no observer is verified against it (`#inScopeGatekeepers`).
- **The kernel calls a named entrypoint of a gadget's `server.js`.** `ExportHandler` (`GADGET_EXPORT_ENTRYPOINT`, `gadget-export.ts`) is found with `loadGadgetWorker(gadgetId).getEntrypoint(…)` in `#resolveGadgetExportFormats`, receives a stub to the gadget's facet as an argument, and has what it returns validated and bounded (`validateExportFormats`, `exportServerFormat`).
- **Observer requirements are the kernel's** (`observer-ledger.ts`; docs/observers.md, "Kernel-checked subresources"). A read that reports `ObservationDescription.subresources` is attributed to its caller's context (`observerContextOf`), checked for every observer that context reaches, and kept on its audit record, which each admission checks again (`ensureObserver`). The check is the connection's `verifyObserverAccess()`, asked with a verifier from the account each observer chose for it (`requireVerifier`). An observer with no such choice is left out (`#audience`), and the owner is never an observer: no read is checked against the owner, whose open runs no admission (`OverseerDurableObject.open()` calls `authorizeCollaborator` for a non-owner only).
- **A space gives roles, and no calls.** The one question it answers about a workspace is which role a profile holds on it (`SpaceModel.workspaceRole`, through `UserDurableObject.workspaceRoleInSpace`). Each answer that gives a role is a `SpaceLease`; when one stops holding, the space's alarm tells the workspace (`OverseerDurableObject.revokeSpaceAccess`), which restarts if it gave that profile a role (`revokeSpaceRole`). Neither `containsRestrictedData` nor `ownerInvitesOnly` changes that: a space lists such a workspace and its members hold their role on it, each verified as an observer like a collaborator with that access (docs/spaces.md, "Access through membership"). `SpaceDurableObject` is called by `AuthenticatedApiImpl` and `UserDurableObject` only, and the Overseer stores nothing about spaces.
- **The platform bounds a chain of calls.** The runtime spends a counter on Durable Object calls and refuses a call once it is exhausted (`isLoopLimitError` in `do-retry.ts`); the repository records no number for it. An exhausted counter can stay with an Overseer instance until the instance is replaced, so `restartIfLoopLimited` restarts a workspace when one of its own calls to a User DO is refused that way, and never for an error that gadget code can throw.

## Proposed decisions

**1. A space binding is an ordinary binding edge whose target is a gatekeeper record the kernel defines.** The record names one gadget of another workspace. Its class, `SpaceBindingGatekeeper`, is exported by the kernel's Worker, and its creation spec is `{type: "spaceBinding", workspaceId, gadgetId}`. It is made as an agent spawner is, through `addGatekeeper`, by a `build` session or at the agent's request through `requestConnection` (as with `AGENT_SPAWNER_CONNECTION_ID`); its creator picks a workspace that A's space lists and a gadget of it that they can open. From there nothing is special: `bindWorkpiece` binds the record, `getEnvForLoader` mints its loopback, and `openGatekeeperSession` gives its sessions a queue of their own, as it gives a spawner's, through which the Overseer, not the facet, makes the call (decision 2).

Everything keyed by a gatekeeper's id then applies with no new case: the action log and its approvals (`ActionRecord.gatekeeperId`, `autoApprovalRule`), the Connections list and unbinding (`GadgetClient.listBindings`, `unbind`), `removeGatekeeper` and `#gadgetReach`. The alternative, an edge that targets B's gadget through a new `BindingLoopbackTarget`, has no id to store: `BindingRecord.target` comes from the workspace's own counter, where B's gadget has none, and each of those mechanisms would need a second answer for it. This is not the gadget-to-gadget edge that plans/multi-gadget.md anticipates, and `bindWorkpiece`'s refusal of a gadget stands.

The cost is a variant of `GatekeeperCreationSpec`, which is public API, and a case wherever the variants are told apart: `collectBindingMetadata` must export it as a binding to fill, since a workspace's id means nothing outside its space, and `connectionTypeFromCreationSpec` must name it.

**2. Every call is resolved through the loopback, and the space confirms it each time.** A call on the binding reaches `startGatekeeperSession` like any other, and the session's queue has A's Overseer make it. A's Overseer first asks its owner's User DO, as it does for a role (`workspaceRoleInSpace`). That User DO answers only for a workspace of its own that has seen activity, and then asks the space its record points at, which says yes only if it lists A under that owner and lists B: a sibling of `SpaceModel.workspaceRole`, and likewise a lease. A's Overseer then calls B's, stating its own id and that space's. Before it serves the call, B's Overseer asks the same through its own owner's record, which must point at the space A named. A personal space counts like any other: it lists its owner's workspaces, so the User DO asks it here, which for a role it never does.

So both pointers and the listing agree, as they must for a role (docs/spaces.md, "Access through membership"), and a listing that trails a move gives no call. An Overseer could instead remember the answer under its lease. It asks every time because a remembered answer would stand until its revocation arrived, and delivery can wait five minutes between attempts (`REVOCATION_RETRY_MS`). Asked each time, a binding ends when the listing does, as an open does.

The cost is two more Durable Object calls on each side of every call, each rewriting a lease, and a dependence on both owners' User DOs. They are calls that `restartIfLoopLimited` watches, and here gadget code drives them (see "Cycles and call depth").

**3. What B exposes is a named entrypoint of its `server.js`.** A gadget can be called from its space only if its `server.js` exports a `WorkerEntrypoint` under one reserved name, `SpaceHandler`, beside `ExportHandler`. B's Overseer loads the gadget's mainline code (`loadGadgetWorker(gadgetId)`, with no chat), takes the entrypoint by name and calls the method A named, passing a stub to the gadget's facet (`getGadgetFacet`) and the caller (decision 4) ahead of A's arguments, as `GadgetExportEntrypoint.export(gadget, id)` receives its gadget. The call runs under a deadline, as `exportServerFormat` applies one (`createExportDeadline`). The size cap of that path counts the bytes of a stream (`limitExportStream`), so a bound on what a handler takes and returns would be new code.

A's agent cannot read B's code, so the gadget also publishes TypeScript declarations for its handler: a file among its accepted files beside `server.js`, which B's Overseer reads without running anything (`readGadgetFiles`). They are captured once, when the record is made. A's Overseer asks B's for them under the check of decision 2 and the flags of decision 6, for a creator who can open the gadget (decision 1), and keeps the text in the record's props, as `AgentSpawnerBindingProps` keeps a spawner's configuration. `Gatekeeper.getTypeScriptTypes()` returns that stored text and asks B nothing. It could not ask under any check: `OverseerImpl.describeGatekeeper` calls it and `describe()` on the facet with no session and no queue, so nothing of decisions 2, 5 and 6 runs there.

The alternative is the gadget's `Gadget` class, which `connectToGadget` and `env.GADGET` reach. Its methods were written for the gadget's own UI and for callers its workspace admitted, and opening them all to every workspace of the space would be a grant nobody made. A separate export makes exposure an act in code, accepted like any other change, and a gadget without one exposes nothing.

The costs: a missing entrypoint is recognized by the text of the runtime's error, as in `readCustomExportFormats`. And the declarations are a flow from B that no observation records. Every `build` session of A, and its agent, reads them whether or not that person could open B's gadget, and they stay as captured until the record is made again. What bounds the flow is its form: bounded text that B's builders accepted into the gadget's code, never what a running handler computes from what B has stored.

**4. The caller is the calling workspace, not a user.** The handler is told which workspace is calling and nothing about a person. A's Overseer states its own id on the call to B's, a plain parameter that is authoritative as those of `OverseerDurableObject.open()` are, and B's confirms it with the space (decision 2). On A's side the call is recorded under the `GatekeeperCaller` its loopback carries, as for any binding. On B's side the handler runs with the env its gadget was loaded with (`getEnvForLoader`), so what it does through B's bindings is B's gadget acting: B's approval queue, B's rules, B's observers.

There is no user to pass. The `gadget` variant of `GatekeeperCaller` names a gadget and at most a chat, and gadget code runs for a hook's firing as it does for a session (`#gitAuthorFor`: "gadget code, with no user behind it"). A profile relayed by A would be a claim made by A's code. And B's answer has to be the same whoever is behind the call, because A keeps it and shows it to its whole audience (decision 5).

The cost: B cannot answer per person. Its handler acts with B's bindings for any workspace the space lists, unless something on B's side says who may call (see "Who consents on B's side"). And B's action log attributes what the handler did to B's gadget. That A asked for it is on A's record only, which is written before the call is made (decision 5): no handler runs for a call that left no record.

**5. What A reads from B is an observation whose subresource is B's gadget.** The gatekeeper reports the read through `authorizeObservation`, with one subresource ID that names B's gadget, before A's Overseer makes the call: the ID is the record's own, known with nothing read yet. `ObserverLedger.authorizeObservation` then does what it does for any such read: it attributes the read to the calling context (`observerContextOf`), checks its audience before the audit record commits, and keeps the ID on that record as a requirement that each later admission reaching the context checks (`observerScope`, `ensureObserver`). Only then does B's handler run. A refused read records nothing (docs/observers.md, "Kernel-checked subresources") and so makes no call, every call B serves is on A's record, and the one check covers whatever comes back, a thrown error included: its message is the handler's to write, and the kernel passes a gadget's error on as thrown (`getGadgetFacet`, `exportServerFormat`).

What the check asks about each person is whether they could open that gadget. B's Overseer answers per profile. B's owner passes. Anyone else has to pass the gate a `use` collaborator passes, `authorizeGadget`: their access in B, the sharing graph's joined with B's own space role (`#accessToOpen`), includes the gadget, and they pass `ensureObserver` for what the gadget reaches, with no configuration prompt. So everyone who can see the context that read was, at the read, someone B would let open the gadget it came from, and everyone A admits to that context afterwards still is: its audience stays within B's, save for A's owner, who is asked about at each read and at no open. That guarantee has two limits, given below, and the second leaves this decision incomplete.

No account stands behind such a read, and two things follow. The answer comes from B's Overseer about profile ids, not from `verifyObserverAccess()` about verifiers. And the audience is everyone to whom the sharing graph or a remembered space role gives the context, with or without an observer record, A's owner included. The ledger checks the owner nowhere else, but A's owner need not be able to open B: a workspace stays in a team space after its owner leaves it, and any `build` session can make the binding. The alternative, copying B's own requirements onto A and asking B's vendors about A's observers, would have those observers choose accounts for connections A does not have.

The first limit is the ledger's own: a requirement holds for the context that recorded it and no further (docs/observers.md, "Direct contexts only"). A read made from a chat is recorded on that chat and checked against `build` collaborators alone (`#audience`), and admission to a gadget reads the workspace's requirements and those of the gadgets it reaches, never a chat's (`observerScope`). Nothing follows what code or the agent then writes. So what A's agent read from B in a chat and wrote into a gadget's files or state reaches that gadget's `use` collaborators, whom A's owner may have invited from outside the space, with no question put to B. With a vendor this limit discloses A's own connected data to A's own invitees. Here it carries B's data past B's audience (see "An agent's access through chat bindings").

The second is that B's answer, as written, need not end. `authorizeGadget` runs `ensureObserver`, which checks every requirement recorded in the contexts the gadget reaches, and under this decision those include what B's gadget recorded through space bindings of its own, each answered by asking another workspace. Once A's gadget has read from B's and B's from A's, in two calls that never nested, the question about anyone who owns neither workspace goes from A to B and back without end. Nothing in `ensureObserver` knows it is already being asked (`MAX_VERIFICATION_PASSES` bounds its re-runs after a local change of scope), so the runtime's counter ends it: that person is refused on both gadgets, at every read and every admission, possibly at a call `restartIfLoopLimited` watches (`#lookUpSpaceRole`, through `ownerUserDo`). With no cycle, a chain from A through B to C still makes each check in A walk B and then C for each person. What B's answer covers is therefore open (see "How far B's answer reaches"), and until it is settled this decision says what is asked and of whom, not what the answer takes.

The costs: the ledger gains a second way to answer a check, and admission must count a connection that needs no account (`#inScopeGatekeepers`). An account choice is the kernel's only record that an admission verified someone for a connection (`ObserverRecord.accountChoices`), and three places read it that way, each of which needs a case for a connection with no account to choose: `ensureObserver` prompts for a connection that has none and throws for one with no vendor, `listObserverRequirements` reports each connection in scope as an account to choose, and `mayReadChatState` lets a chat's title and latest-message preview go to a `build` collaborator in the background (`deliverDirtyChats`, `headForMessage`) only if every connection in scope has one. For that fanout the case is to leave the binding out. Counted, it would stop the fanout for every collaborator. Left out, it adds no reader: a collaborator's User DO keeps a packet only once it lists the workspace (`UserDurableObject.messageArrived`), their first open is what writes that listing (`recordSharedWorkspaceOpen`), and that open is an admission that asks B. Asking B per message is ruled out (packages/workshop-backend/AGENTS.md). A record written ahead of the call stands when the call fails or returns nothing, so its requirement can ask for more than was read. Each read is at least one admission in B for every member of A's audience. Someone who has not chosen accounts for what B's gadget reaches is refused, and A's read with them, until they open that gadget once. Each member B is asked about leaves a lease on B (`#lookUpSpaceRole`), so removing them from the space restarts B as well as A. The requirement outlives the binding, so A's admissions go on asking B after the space has stopped listing either of them (see "What a requirement asks once the binding has ended"). As with a vendor, someone B stops admitting keeps their session on A until their next admission there. A's owner has none: once B stops admitting them, every further read through the binding is refused on their account, and they keep for good what A already holds from B's gadget. Closing that would take an admission at the owner's open, which could refuse an owner their own workspace and, with it, `Overseer.deleteSelf` and `Overseer.moveToSpace`, which nobody else may call.

**6. A restricted workspace is neither caller nor callee.** A workspace under `containsRestrictedData` or `ownerInvitesOnly` makes no call through a space binding and serves none. Its Overseer decides that from its own two flags, asking nobody, from the write that sets one. The space is no help here: it is not told the flags and lists such a workspace like any other, so decision 2 says yes for it.

As a caller, a workspace that holds restricted data would send out in its arguments what restricted mode keeps in: there no rule approves an action (`autoApprovalRule`): `submitAction` refuses a git push outright and holds every other action for a person, and web fetches are held to hosts an admin trusts or URLs a user approves (`authorizeWebFetch`). As a callee, the alternative is to let the flags travel with the read, as they do on a vendor's observation. A would then be restricted by its first read from B and, as a caller, make no second call: the binding would end itself. An owner-invites-only workspace is out because that flag is a promise to a data source that each person who reaches the data was let in one at a time, and a call has no person: until decision 5 is complete, nothing checks the caller's audience against the callee's.

The cost: both flags are permanent, so a binding that works stops for good when either side first observes restricted data, and the workspaces that hold the most sensitive data are the ones that cannot be composed. It is also stricter than the space's rule for people, who open a workspace under either flag once verified as observers: a complete decision 5 would be that verification for a call, and this decision could then be relaxed to match.

**7. Revocation runs through the space's lease queue.** A `SpaceLease` gains a second kind of holder: beside (workspace, profile), a (workspace, peer workspace) pair, written by each yes of decision 2. `detachWorkspace` revokes the leases on the workspace it drops, as it does for roles, and also those that name it as the peer, through an index like `byProfile`. So when either workspace leaves the listing, whatever took it off (a move, a deletion, a flag), each Overseer the space told yes hears of it: through the same `revocations` queue and the same alarm, by a call beside `revokeSpaceAccess`.

On arrival an Overseer stops trusting what is in flight for that peer, as `#spaceRolesTaken` does for a lookup, so a call under way fails where it would have returned. It restarts, as `revokeSpaceRole` does, only if it handed that peer something that outlives a call, which depends on "Passing stubs and hooks across the boundary".

The alternatives are a second queue, or Overseers that ask again on a timer. This is one mechanism, stored with the change that causes it and retried until delivered, and no Overseer is polled. The cost: `SpaceDurableObject.alarm` delivers two kinds of revocation; a workspace that leaves queues one for every lease standing, since leases are only ever revoked; and a call in flight can outlive the listing for as long as a member's session can (docs/spaces.md, "Known limitations").

## Open questions

Each option is listed with what it would commit the kernel to.

**Who consents on B's side.** Whether exporting `SpaceHandler` is all the consent B gives.
- *The export is the consent.* Any workspace the space lists may bind the gadget. Commits to nothing more. Who can have a listed workspace call is whoever changes or steers its code: its owner, everyone who holds `build` on it, through the space or through its own sharing, which reaches people outside the space, and its agent. Any member, `use` included, may add a workspace of their own to a team space (`SpaceModel.canAddWorkspaces`), and it stays listed after its owner leaves or is removed (docs/spaces.md, "A workspace cannot be evicted"; `SpaceModel.attachWorkspaces` keeps an entry under the same owner), which decision 2 does not see: it checks listings and pointers, never membership. What limits the calls is the check of decision 5, made before each one. B's handler runs only for a context all of whose audience B would let open the gadget, A's owner included, so the workspace of a removed member calls only if B's own sharing still admits them. Within that, nothing bounds what the handler changes, and B keeps no record of which workspace asked (decision 4).
- *B's owner or builders name the callers.* A grant per calling workspace in B's Overseer, checked where B serves the call. Commits to a record beside the sharing graph, its UI, and ending calls when a grant is withdrawn.
- *A space admin approves each edge.* Commits the space to storing edges, where for a binding it stores only leases (decision 7), and gives the `admin` role its first power over a workspace that a `build` member lacks. On the workspaces a space lists, an admin holds `build` and no more (`workspaceRoleOf`), and so can already make or remove a binding, as any builder can; what the role adds is changing the member list (`SpaceModel.setMemberRole`, `removeMember`). Membership gives nobody the power to share a workspace, and an admin cannot take one off the listing (docs/spaces.md).

**What "the same space" means once spaces nest.** docs/spaces.md has spaces as a flat list. Suppose spaces come to nest, a sub-space inheriting its parent's members and adding its own; that is a separate proposal and no part of this branch. Which pairs of workspaces may a binding then join?
- *Those one space lists.* Decision 2 as written. Commits to nothing; a workspace of a sub-space cannot bind one of its parent.
- *Those in one line of descent, or under one root.* Commits the check to walk the chain of spaces (a Durable Object call per level, or each space copying its ancestors' listings), to say which space holds the lease, and to revoke when a sub-space is moved.

Decision 5 limits what the wider option buys. Under that rule a sub-space's members are its parent's and its own, so a workspace of the parent reads from one of the sub-space, and the reverse is refused for each member the sub-space has of its own.

**An agent's access through chat bindings.** A new chat's bindings are seeded from `defaultBindingList()`, which takes every permanent edge of every gadget. The agent would therefore hold each space binding as `env.NAME` and call B from `executeCode` as `{from: "agent", chatId}`.
- *As any binding.* Nothing to add: the read is the chat's, checked against A's `build` collaborators and its owner, under the execution fence (`isLiveExecution`). An agent steered by what it read then calls another workspace with A's standing, and can send it whatever the chat holds. And a chat's read is where the first limit of decision 5 is widest: no `use` collaborator of A is asked about, whichever gadget the agent then writes B's data into.
- *Gadget code only.* The Overseer refuses a caller that is not a gadget, as `startGatekeeperSession` refuses a `worktree` target to any caller but the agent, and the seed leaves the binding out. Commits to that check and that filter. The agent still exercises the binding through the gadget that holds it (`env.NAME.rpc`), where the read is the gadget's, checked against the `use` collaborators whose gadgets reach it (`gadgetsReaching`). That narrows the limit and does not close it: what the gadget returns to the agent carries no requirement into another gadget either.
- *Asked for in each chat.* Left out of the seed, and a chat gets it through `requestConnection`, so a person decides. Commits to the filter only. A chat that has it reads as under the first option.

An agent spawner's edges raise the same question for the agents it starts (`AgentSpawnerQueueImpl`).

**Passing stubs and hooks across the boundary.** Workers RPC carries stubs in arguments and results, and gadget code can mint stubs that persist (`ctx.restore()`, which `loadGadgetWorker` enables with `allow_irrevocable_stub_storage`). A stub that crosses is a capability that the check of decision 2, the audit record and the observer check never see again.
- *Plain data only.* The gatekeeper passes values and refuses the rest. Commits to that filter. A revocation then has only calls in flight to end (decision 7), and there are no callbacks or subscriptions.
- *Stubs that live as long as a session.* Commits to restarting the side that handed one out when its lease ends, and either to wrapping every such stub so that its calls are checked and recorded, or to accepting that they are not.
- *Hooks.* B wakes A's gadget through `ApprovalQueue.bindHook`: a `BoundHookRecord` on A that a user enables, revalidated at each delivery (`requireLiveHook`), its firings attributed to the gadget it wakes. Commits the kernel's gatekeeper to a `HookController`, B to keeping its subscribers and dropping them when the lease ends, and the hook path to serving a connection with no vendor, which `OverseerDurableObject.startHook` refuses today ("Hook vendor is unavailable.").

**Cycles and call depth.** A binds B and B binds A, or a chain runs from A through B to C. Two kinds of chain follow such edges. A handler that makes a call of its own adds to one request's chain at least the call between the two Overseers and the callee's question to its owner's User DO and its space, against the counter described under "Current state". And the check of decision 5 follows the requirements that calls left, at every read and every admission, whether or not any handler ever made one. The options below are about the first kind, and each says what it does for the second, which "How far B's answer reaches" has to bound.
- *The platform's limit alone.* Commits to nothing. A chain that is too long is refused somewhere along it, possibly at a question of decision 2, which is an Overseer's own call to a User DO (`ownerUserDo`): the refusal that `restartIfLoopLimited` restarts a workspace for. Gadget code could then restart a shared workspace, which that function is written to prevent. A cycle of requirements ends only this way, at each check.
- *One hop.* An Overseer refuses a space-binding call from a gadget while that gadget's handler serves one. Commits to tracking calls in flight. It is coarse: no handler can compose other workspaces, and a gadget's unrelated calls are refused meanwhile. It does nothing for checks: two calls that never nested leave a cycle of requirements.
- *No cycle among the edges.* The space refuses a lease that would close one. Commits the space to holding every edge, and bounds no chain's length. It keeps requirements from forming a cycle only if an edge is held for as long as its requirement is read, which is the life of the binding's gatekeeper record and not of a lease (see "What a requirement asks once the binding has ended"): the space would hear of each record made and removed.

The kernel cannot carry a hop count through a handler: only the runtime links an incoming call to the calls the handler then makes.

**Cost attribution.** A call can make B spend. A handler that starts an agent through B's spawner runs a model resolved from the account of that spawner's creator (`AgentSpawnerBindingProps.creatorUserId`), counted in B's totals (`#addChatCost`; display accounting, as `#addRequestCost` says, and no source for billing) and held to B's owner's limit (`checkUsageAndBalance`).
- *B pays.* Commits to nothing: it is what those mechanisms do. Any workspace the space lists can then spend what B's builders connected, which bears on who consents.
- *A pays.* Commits the kernel to tying what a handler spends to the call that caused it, the link it lacks (see "Cycles and call depth"), and to running a model that A's side chose.

Keeping models from a handler is not an option as proposed: the handler holds its gadget's stub, and the gadget has its whole env.

**Talking to the space itself (events, shared data).** A workspace's code, a gadget's or an agent's, cannot ask its space anything: which workspaces it lists, when one joins, a value all of them share. The one question a space answers about a workspace (see "Current state") is its Overseer's own.
- *It stays that way.* Shared data lives in one workspace, and the others bind it. Commits to nothing.
- *A workspace the space designates.* The space names one of its workspaces as the shared one, reached with the binding above. Commits to that pointer and to who may set it.
- *A gatekeeper for the space.* A second kernel-defined record, whose session reads the listing, holds shared values and delivers events. Commits the space to gadget code as a caller, against its trust boundary (docs/spaces.md, "Trust boundary"); to storage of its own, with limits; to an audience rule for what it holds; and to delivering events from an alarm that delivers only revocations.

**Whether a call is a read or an action.** The code forces this question. `Gatekeeper.startSession` requires every operation to be one or the other: a read is authorized before its data returns, and a write waits in the approval queue. A call into B's code can be both, and the kernel cannot tell which from its name. Decision 5 puts every call behind its check and covers what comes back; what A sends is undecided.
- *Every call is a read.* Nothing A sends waits for an approver, as nothing sent to a gadget of one's own workspace does: `startGatekeeperSession` hands a gadget target to `getGadgetFacet` with no action record. Commits to nothing: decision 6 keeps restricted workspaces out, and what B then does outside itself waits in B's own queue.
- *The handler declares.* It marks each method a read or an action, and an action is a `submitAction` on A, under A's auto-approval rules, applied once approved. Commits to a declaration the kernel validates, to action kinds that come from B's code, and to results that arrive after the call that asked for them has returned.
- *Every call is an action.* The same with no declaration, and no call returns its value unless a rule approves it at once.

**How far B's answer reaches.** Decision 5 forces this question and is incomplete without an answer. `authorizeGadget` as it stands answers for everything B's gadget has read, what it read from other workspaces included, by asking them in turn, which does not end on a cycle of requirements (decision 5).
- *One level.* B answers from the person's access to the gadget and from its vendors' requirements, leaving out what the gadget recorded through space bindings. Commits `ensureObserver` to a narrower scope when another workspace asks. Each check is one question to B per person. The guarantee of decision 5 then stops at one hop: what B's gadget holds from C reaches A's audience with C never asked.
- *The read names everything.* Before the call, B's Overseer tells A's which gadgets of other workspaces its gadget has recorded requirements on, and A's read reports them all as subresources, each asked one level deep. Commits B to disclosing those ids to A's Overseer (clients' action-log entries omit `subresources`), A to one question per workspace named, and a requirement to growing with every hop (`MAX_SUBRESOURCES`). A handler that reads from another workspace while it serves adds to the list after A's check, so A has to check the additions before the result returns. Checks end with no protocol between Overseers, and the guarantee holds across hops as of each read.
- *The question carries what is being asked.* Each Overseer passes on the gadgets already under question and takes one of them as passed. Commits to that protocol between Overseers, and to a chain per person as long as the distinct gadgets involved, against the runtime's counter (see "Cycles and call depth").

**What a requirement asks once the binding has ended.** The code forces this question too. The requirement of decision 5 outlives the binding: its record is in a log that is append-only (`ActionRecord`), and admission reads it for as long as the binding's gatekeeper record exists, whether or not a gadget still binds it (`ObserverLedger.observerScope`, `gadgetProducers`). So after either workspace has left the listing, each admission to A that reaches the reading context still asks B. Decisions 2 and 6 have B serve no call by then, and do not say whether this question is one.
- *B still answers.* Commits B's Overseer to a question it answers with no space confirming the pair, and A's admissions to depending on B for as long as the record exists. B answers as it then is: after a move, by its own sharing and the space it belongs to; under a flag, by its own sharing alone (`#lookUpSpaceRole`). A deleted B refuses every open (`OverseerDurableObject.open()`), so it passes nobody.
- *B refuses, as it refuses a call.* Commits to nothing. Every check fails closed (`observer-ledger.ts`), so every non-owner whose grant reaches the reading context, which a `build` grant always does, is refused at their next admission to A. That lasts until A's owner, or a `build` session still open, removes the binding's gatekeeper record (`removeGatekeeper`), which drops the check while what A read stays (docs/observers.md, "Kernel-checked subresources").
